# Persona: Light Novel Reader

## Description

The Light Novel Reader is someone who wants to read Japanese light novels — isekai, fantasy, romance, slice-of-life — in the original language. They're tired of waiting for translations, frustrated by fan translations that miss nuance, or simply want to experience the author's actual voice. They likely already consume anime and manga and have absorbed more Japanese than they realize through passive exposure.

This persona doesn't need business Japanese, academic Japanese, or formal letter writing. They need narrative Japanese: the language of storytelling, dialogue, inner monologue, and worldbuilding. They need to parse colloquial speech, understand character voice through register shifts, and read kanji-dense prose at a pace that doesn't destroy the reading experience.

## Target Proficiency

**JLPT Approximate Level:** N3

This is the inflection point where a reader can start engaging with native material using a dictionary as a crutch rather than a lifeline. N3 grammar covers the conditional forms, giving/receiving constructions, relative clauses, and formal/informal register distinctions that are the backbone of light novel prose.

## Vocabulary Scope

**Target:** ~5,000 words

**Priority domains (in order):**
1. **Narrative vocabulary** — verbs of motion, perception, emotion, transformation. The words that move a story forward: aruku, hashiru, kizuku, omou, kawaru, naru, tsuzuku.
2. **Dialogue vocabulary** — casual speech, interjections, sentence-final particles, filler words. The words that make characters sound human: maji, yabai, sore wa, naa, sa, kke.
3. **Fantasy/genre vocabulary** — maou (demon lord), yuusha (hero), bouken (adventure), guild, quest, skill, level. The genre-specific lexicon that shows up on every page.
4. **Emotional/psychological vocabulary** — fuan (anxiety), kakugo (resolve), koukai (regret), kitai (expectation). Light novels live in the protagonist's head.
5. **Descriptive vocabulary** — physical descriptions, weather, settings, time expressions. The connective tissue of prose.
6. **Daily life vocabulary** — food, clothing, travel, commerce. Background knowledge that supports scene comprehension.

**Explicitly deprioritized:**
- Business vocabulary (keigo beyond basic desu/masu)
- Academic/scientific vocabulary
- Legal/bureaucratic vocabulary
- Archaic/literary Japanese (beyond common light novel archaisms)

## Kanji Scope

**Target:** ~800 characters

**Strategy:** Kanji are taught through vocabulary, never in isolation. A kanji is introduced when the student encounters a word that uses it. Radical knowledge is built inductively — "you've now seen this component in three different kanji; it usually suggests [meaning/reading]."

**Priority tiers:**
1. **High-frequency narrative kanji (~300):** The characters that appear on nearly every page. 人, 大, 出, 行, 見, 思, 言, 来, 手, 中, 目, 力, 気, 魔, 剣, 王, etc.
2. **Supporting kanji (~300):** Characters that appear frequently but not constantly. Includes kanji for common verbs, adjectives, and genre terms.
3. **Recognition kanji (~200):** Characters the student should be able to read in context but doesn't need to write. Includes less common genre vocabulary and literary terms.

**Reading vs. writing:** The persona is optimized for reading. Writing (production of kanji) is secondary. The student should recognize kanji in context and know their readings; handwriting is not a goal.

## Grammar Scope

**Coverage:** N5 through N3 grammar points, with selective N2 patterns that appear frequently in light novels.

**N5 foundation (~80 patterns):**
- Basic sentence structure (X wa Y desu, X ga arimasu/imasu)
- Particles (wa, ga, wo, ni, de, e, to, mo, ka, no, ya)
- Te-form and its basic uses (sequential, request, progressive)
- Basic adjective conjugation (i-adjectives, na-adjectives)
- Past tense, negative, past-negative
- Basic counters and time expressions

**N4 expansion (~120 patterns):**
- Potential form, volitional form, imperative form
- Giving/receiving (ageru, morau, kureru and their te-form extensions)
- Conditional forms (tara, ba, to, nara)
- Passive voice, causative voice
- Relative clauses
- Conjunctions and compound sentences (shi, kedo, noni, node)
- Basic honorifics and humble forms

**N3 target (~100 patterns):**
- Causative-passive
- Advanced te-form auxiliaries (shimau, oku, miru, iku, kuru)
- Nominal and adverbial clauses
- Advanced conditionals (toshitara, to shitemo, nara...ga)
- Expressing hearsay, conjecture, appearance (rashii, you, mitai, sou)
- Formal written patterns common in narration (ni oite, ni totte, ni tsuite)
- Sentence-ending particles and their pragmatic force

**Selective N2 patterns (~30):**
- Patterns common in light novel narration: ~ba~hodo, ~tsutsu, ~kagiri, ~wari ni
- Patterns common in character speech: ~tte ba, ~nda tte, ~kke
- Written narrative connectors: ~ta totan, ~ya ina ya, ~ka to omou to

## Key Skills

1. **Reading comprehension** — Parse multi-clause sentences with relative clauses, conditional chains, and embedded quotes. Follow paragraph-level narrative flow without losing the thread.

2. **Colloquial speech pattern recognition** — Understand contracted forms (janakereba -> janakya), masculine/feminine speech markers, dialect hints, and the casual register that dominates light novel dialogue.

3. **Narrative context inference** — Use genre knowledge to fill gaps. When a character says "ore wa..." in a tense battle scene, the student can infer tone and register without needing every word translated.

4. **Register recognition** — Identify when a character shifts between casual, polite, and formal registers, and understand what that shift signals about the social dynamics of the scene.

5. **Kanji compound decoding** — Break unknown compound words into known kanji components to guess meaning. Essential for the frequent genre-specific coinages in fantasy light novels.

## Corpus Sources

- **Light novel text:** Published isekai, fantasy, and slice-of-life light novels (Sword Art Online, Re:Zero, Konosuba, Mushoku Tensei, etc.)
- **Anime subtitles:** Japanese subtitles/closed captions for anime adaptations, providing spoken Japanese aligned to visual context
- **Manga dialogue:** Raw manga dialogue providing casual spoken Japanese in visual context
- **Web novels (narou):** Syosetu ni Narou web novels, which are the raw precursors to published light novels and tend to use simpler language
- **Visual novel scripts:** Dialogue-heavy interactive fiction with clear speaker attribution and emotional context

## Definition of Done

The student **can read a typical isekai light novel** — Sword Art Online, Konosuba, Mushoku Tensei, or equivalent — **with occasional dictionary lookups** for unknown vocabulary, but without needing to stop and analyze grammar.

Concretely:
- Can read a page of light novel prose in under 5 minutes
- Looks up fewer than 3 words per page on average
- Can follow dialogue without losing track of who is speaking
- Understands the emotional tone of scenes (comedy, tension, melancholy) from the Japanese, not from context alone
- Can identify when a character is being sarcastic, formal, casual, or deceptive based on language choices
- Can read furigana-less kanji for the 800 target characters in context
- Can enjoy the reading experience — not just decode it

The student is NOT expected to:
- Write light-novel-quality prose
- Speak Japanese fluently in real-time conversation
- Pass the JLPT N3 (the persona optimizes for reading comprehension, not test performance)
- Read literary fiction, newspapers, or academic text
- Understand heavy dialect or period Japanese
