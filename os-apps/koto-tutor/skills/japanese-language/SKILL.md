# Skill: Japanese Language Teaching

## Purpose

This skill encodes Sensei's domain expertise in Japanese linguistics and the specific strategies for teaching each major area of the language to English-speaking learners.

## Particles

Particles are the skeleton of Japanese. English speakers struggle because particles encode spatial, logical, and pragmatic relationships that English handles through word order, prepositions, or context.

### Teaching Strategy

Never teach particles as a list. Teach them as answers to specific questions:

- **wa** — "What are we talking about?" (topic marker, not subject)
- **ga** — "What/who is doing/being the thing?" (subject/focus marker)
- **wo** — "What is being acted upon?" (direct object)
- **ni** — "Where is the destination/target/time point?" (goal, location of existence, time)
- **de** — "Where/how is the action happening?" (location of action, means, material)
- **e** — "Which direction?" (directional, often interchangeable with ni for motion)
- **to** — "With whom / what's the complete list?" (accompaniment, exhaustive listing, quotation)
- **mo** — "This too?" (additive)
- **no** — "Whose / which kind?" (possession, modification, nominalization)
- **ka** — "Is this a question?" (question marker)
- **ya** — "And others like this?" (non-exhaustive listing)

### The wa/ga Problem

This is the single most confusing distinction for English speakers. Do not teach it as "topic vs. subject" — that's technically correct but pedagogically useless until the student understands what "topic" means in Japanese information structure.

**Strategy:** Teach through contrast pairs.

1. "Taro wa gakusei desu." — (Speaking of Taro, he's a student.) We already know who Taro is.
2. "Taro ga gakusei desu." — (It's Taro who's a student.) Someone asked "who's the student?"

Build the distinction through scenes: "Someone knocked. Who is it? [ga] vs. I want to tell you about my friend. [wa]"

The student will take months to internalize this. That's normal. Keep surfacing it in different contexts. Every time they get it right, point out why. Every time they hesitate, that's growth.

### Particle Stacking

Particles combine: ni wa, de wa, to wa, kara wa, made ni. Teach these as they arise naturally, not as a separate topic. When the student encounters "kono machi ni wa" — explain that ni marks the location and wa marks it as the topic. Two jobs, two particles.

## Kanji and Radicals

### Teaching Strategy

Kanji are taught through vocabulary, never in isolation. The sequence:

1. Student encounters a word in context (e.g., "taberu" in a scene about the guild cafeteria)
2. Sensei shows the kanji: 食べる
3. If the student has seen the radical before (食 contains 人 and 良-like components), connect it
4. If not, introduce the radical meaning as a mnemonic hook, not a rule
5. The kanji is "learned" when the student recognizes it in 3+ different words across different encounters

### Radical Strategy

Radicals are pattern recognition tools, not a prerequisite. Teach them inductively:

- After the student has seen 人 in 人, 休, 体, 住 — point out the person radical
- After 水/氵 appears in 海, 池, 泳, 洗 — point out the water radical
- Let the student start predicting: "This has the fire radical... is it related to heat?"

### Readings: On'yomi vs. Kun'yomi

Don't teach this distinction explicitly until the student asks or until it causes confusion. Practically:

- Single kanji + okurigana = usually kun'yomi (食べる = taberu)
- Compound kanji = usually on'yomi (食事 = shokuji)
- Exceptions are common; learn them as vocabulary, not as rules

## Verb Conjugation

### Teaching Strategy

Japanese verb conjugation is highly regular. English speakers expect irregularity (because English verbs are irregular). Lean into the regularity.

**Group classification:**
- **Godan (Group 1):** Consonant-stem verbs. The stem ends in a consonant that changes. Teach the vowel row pattern (a, i, u, e, o).
- **Ichidan (Group 2):** Vowel-stem verbs. Drop -ru, add the suffix. Simple.
- **Irregular:** suru and kuru. Just two. Memorize them.

**Conjugation teaching order (by utility, not textbook order):**
1. Masu-form (polite present) — immediate practical use
2. Te-form — unlocks requests, progressive, sequential, and 10+ auxiliary patterns
3. Nai-form (negative) — essential for daily conversation
4. Ta-form (past) — same pattern as te-form, so it's nearly free
5. Dictionary form — for casual speech and subordinate clauses
6. Potential form — "can do" is highly motivating
7. Volitional form — "let's" / "I'll"
8. Conditional forms (ba, tara) — unlocked together
9. Passive — reading comprehension essential
10. Causative — less common but appears in light novels
11. Causative-passive — the boss fight of conjugation

**Te-form is the keystone.** Invest heavily in te-form mastery. It unlocks:
- Requests (te kudasai)
- Progressive (te iru)
- Sequential actions (te, te, te...)
- Giving/receiving (te ageru, te morau, te kureru)
- Completion/regret (te shimau)
- Preparation (te oku)
- Trying (te miru)
- Directional (te iku, te kuru)

### Common Pitfalls for English Speakers

- **Tense confusion:** Japanese "present tense" is really non-past. It covers present AND future. Students add unnecessary future markers.
- **Negative scope:** Japanese negation attaches to the verb, but the scope can be wider than English speakers expect. "Amari tabenakatta" isn't "I somewhat didn't eat" — it's "I didn't eat much."
- **Stative vs. resultative te iru:** "Kekkon shite iru" means "is married" (resultative state), not "is in the process of marrying" (progressive). English speakers default to progressive interpretation.

## Honorifics and Register

### Teaching Strategy

Teach register as character voice, not as social rules. Light novel readers encounter register shifts constantly — they're a storytelling device, not just politeness.

**Levels (introduced progressively):**
1. **Casual (tameguchi):** ore/boku/atashi, plain forms, sentence-final particles. This is where light novel dialogue lives.
2. **Polite (teineigo):** desu/masu forms. The "default" register for talking to strangers.
3. **Respectful (sonkeigo):** Elevating the other person's actions. Appears when characters address authority figures.
4. **Humble (kenjougo):** Lowering your own actions. Appears in formal scenes and period pieces.

**For light novel readers specifically:**
- First-person pronoun choice (ore, boku, watashi, atashi, uchi, washi) is character identity. Teach it as characterization.
- Sentence-final particles (na, ne, yo, ze, wa, no, sa, kke) are emotional coloring. A sentence with yo vs. without yo is a different sentence.
- Register shifts mid-conversation signal power dynamics, emotional changes, or comedic beats. Train the student to notice them.

### Common Pitfalls

- **Over-formality:** English speakers default to polite forms because they're taught first. In light novel dialogue, casual forms are the norm. Push students toward casual production earlier than textbooks would.
- **Pronoun rigidity:** English speakers pick one pronoun and stick with it. Japanese speakers shift pronouns based on context. The same character might use ore with friends and watashi with a teacher.
- **Keigo paralysis:** Students freeze when they think honorifics might be needed. Teach the principle (who is higher status?) and a few key forms, not the full system.

## Sentence Structure

### The SOV Myth

Japanese is technically SOV (Subject-Object-Verb), but that framing is misleading. Better framing:

**Japanese is verb-final with free word order for everything else.**

Particles mark roles, so word order is flexible. "Taro ga ringo wo tabeta" and "ringo wo Taro ga tabeta" are both grammatical — they differ in emphasis, not meaning. Teach this early; it prevents the student from trying to parse Japanese with English word-order assumptions.

### Information Structure

Japanese organizes information as: known/old information first, new/important information at the end (before the verb). This is why wa (topic = known) tends to come first and ga (subject = potentially new) can come later.

This is the deep insight that unlocks Japanese reading comprehension. Students who understand information structure stop struggling with long sentences.

### Subordinate Clause Stacking

Japanese builds complex meaning by stacking clauses before the main verb. Light novels love this:

"Maou ga hametsu no jumon wo tonaeta shunkan, yuusha wa ken wo nuki..."

English speakers need to learn to hold subordinate clauses in memory while waiting for the main clause. Practice with increasingly complex stacks, always in narrative context.
