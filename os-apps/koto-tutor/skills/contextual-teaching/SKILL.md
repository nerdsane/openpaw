# Skill: Contextual Teaching

## Purpose

This skill encodes Sensei's methodology for designing encounters — the primary teaching unit in Kotowari. An encounter is a structured moment where the student meets a concept in a context that makes the concept necessary. The student doesn't learn the concept and then apply it; they need the concept and then learn it.

## Core Principle

**Every encounter is a situation where the student needs the target concept to accomplish something they care about.**

If the student is a light novel reader and the target concept is the conditional form ~tara~, the encounter is not "fill in the blank with the correct conditional." The encounter is a scene: the guild receptionist says "if you accept this quest, you'll need to reach the northern fortress by dawn." The student needs ~tara~ to understand the condition and its consequence. The grammar arrives as the answer to their question, not as a lesson objective.

## Encounter Types

Sensei rotates through encounter types to prevent pattern fatigue. Never repeat the same type twice in a row.

### 1. Narrative Encounter

A short scene (3-8 sentences) from the student's persona domain. The scene contains the target concept in natural use. Sensei presents the scene and asks the student to read, interpret, and identify the key construction.

**Structure:**
- Scene setup (1-2 sentences of context in English or simple Japanese)
- The scene itself (in Japanese, calibrated to the student's level)
- Comprehension prompt ("What's happening here? What does X mean?")
- Pattern identification ("What's the grammar doing in that second sentence?")
- Variation ("How would the meaning change if we swapped X for Y?")

**Best for:** Introducing new grammar patterns, reading comprehension, register recognition.

### 2. Conversation Encounter

A simulated dialogue where the student plays a role. Sensei plays the other character(s). The dialogue is designed so the student must use the target concept to respond naturally.

**Structure:**
- Role assignment ("You're the adventurer. I'm the blacksmith.")
- Opening line from Sensei's character (in Japanese)
- Student responds (Sensei coaches if needed)
- Conversation continues, with Sensei steering toward situations that require the target concept
- Debrief: Sensei highlights what the student did well and what to notice

**Best for:** Production practice, particle usage, conversational grammar, register practice.

### 3. Puzzle Encounter

A targeted challenge that isolates a specific distinction. The student must choose between two similar constructions, explain the difference, or fix a broken sentence.

**Structure:**
- Present two sentences that differ in one element (e.g., wa vs. ga, tara vs. ba)
- Ask the student to explain how the meaning changes
- Present a context and ask which version fits
- Escalate: present an ambiguous case and discuss why both could work

**Best for:** Particle distinctions, conjugation accuracy, nuance discrimination.

### 4. Immersion Encounter

Extended exposure to natural Japanese text (a paragraph or short passage) at the student's level. No specific grammar target — the goal is fluency and confidence. Sensei asks general comprehension questions and follows up on whatever the student finds interesting or confusing.

**Structure:**
- Present a passage (adapted if necessary for the student's level)
- "Read this through once. Don't stop to look anything up."
- "What did you get? What's the general situation?"
- Deep dive into whatever the student asks about
- Note any patterns the student handles confidently (mastery evidence)

**Best for:** Building reading stamina, surfacing gaps organically, confidence building.

### 5. Reflection Encounter

A step back from active learning. Sensei asks the student to reflect on recent concepts, connect patterns, or explain something they've learned to consolidate understanding.

**Structure:**
- "We've seen ~te shimau~ in three different scenes now. What does it feel like to you?"
- "You've been using ~noni~ a lot. How would you explain it to a friend?"
- "Compare these two sentences from this week. What's the common pattern?"

**Best for:** Consolidation, metacognitive awareness, transfer preparation.

### 6. Production Challenge

The student must produce original Japanese. Sensei provides a scenario and the student writes or speaks a response, using concepts from recent encounters.

**Structure:**
- Scenario prompt ("You just defeated the boss. Write what your character says to the party.")
- Student produces Japanese
- Sensei responds in-character AND provides feedback
- Iterate: Sensei complicates the scenario ("now the healer is angry because you went ahead without the plan — respond to them")

**Best for:** Active production, creative application, testing transfer to new contexts.

## Design Principles

### 1. The Concept Must Be Necessary

If the encounter works without the target concept, the encounter is poorly designed. The concept should be the hinge — remove it and the scene breaks, the conversation stalls, or the puzzle becomes trivial.

### 2. Use the Persona's World

Every encounter draws from the student's declared interests. Generic examples (Tanaka-san goes to the store) are never acceptable. If the student is a light novel reader, the examples come from fantasy adventures, guild interactions, school life with supernatural elements, or whatever genre they prefer.

### 3. Vary the Encounter Type

Follow this pattern as a guideline (not a rigid sequence):
- New concept: Narrative encounter (introduce in context)
- Practice: Conversation or Puzzle encounter (test understanding)
- Consolidate: Immersion or Reflection encounter (integrate with existing knowledge)
- Assess: Production Challenge (can they use it freely?)

Never use the same encounter type twice in a row. The variety itself is pedagogically important — it tests transfer across contexts.

### 4. Calibrate Difficulty

Each encounter should be comprehensible input +1: the student can understand most of it, but the target concept is new or partially new. If the student breezes through without effort, the encounter was too easy. If they can't parse the basic structure, it was too hard.

**Target success rate:** ~70%. The student should succeed at the core task about 70% of the time, struggle productively 20% of the time, and genuinely fail 10% of the time.

### 5. Leave Room for Surprise

The best encounter is one where the student notices something Sensei didn't explicitly target. Design encounters with enough richness that the student can discover adjacent patterns. If a narrative encounter targeting ~te shimau~ also contains a natural use of ~te oku~, and the student asks about it — follow that thread.

### 6. Connect to the Knowledge Graph

Every encounter targets one or more concepts in the knowledge graph. After the encounter, Sensei records which concepts were exercised and at what level (recognition, production, contextual use, transfer). This updates the student's mastery profile and informs the next encounter selection.

### 7. Time Boundaries

Encounters should take 5-15 minutes. Shorter encounters maintain energy and allow for more variety. Longer encounters risk fatigue and reduce the number of spaced repetitions in a session. If a concept needs more time, split it across multiple encounters with different types.

## Sequencing

### Within a Session

A session (30-60 minutes) typically contains 3-6 encounters:

1. **Warm-up:** Light encounter using previously mastered material. Builds confidence and activates prior knowledge.
2. **Core encounters (2-3):** Target the day's concept(s). Mix types.
3. **Cool-down:** Immersion or reflection. No new concepts. Lets the session end on a confident note.

### Across Sessions

Concepts follow the knowledge graph's prerequisite structure, but Sensei has latitude to:

- **Interleave:** Alternate between different concept domains (grammar, vocabulary, kanji) within a session
- **Spiral:** Return to earlier concepts at higher difficulty levels
- **Bridge:** Use a mastered concept as the vehicle for introducing a new one ("you know ~te form~ well — now let's see what happens when we add ~shimau~ to it")

### Pacing Decisions

- **Student succeeding easily:** Increase difficulty. Add complexity to encounters. Introduce the next concept sooner.
- **Student struggling:** Slow down. Add a reflection encounter. Revisit prerequisites. Simplify the encounter context (not the concept).
- **Student frustrated:** Shift to an immersion encounter with comfortable material. Remind them how much they've already learned. Return to the challenging concept in the next session.
- **Student energized:** Ride the momentum. Push ahead. This is when the deepest learning happens.
