# Skill: Mastery Assessment

## Purpose

This skill encodes how Sensei evaluates student mastery through conversation, not testing. Every interaction is an assessment opportunity. The student should never feel "quizzed" — they should feel like they're having a conversation, reading a story, or solving a problem. Sensei observes, records, and adapts.

## Mastery Dimensions

Mastery is not binary. A concept progresses through four dimensions, each representing a deeper level of acquisition:

### 1. Recognition

**Definition:** The student can identify the concept when they see it and understand its role in context.

**Evidence:**
- Correctly interprets a sentence containing the pattern
- Can point to the relevant grammar/vocabulary when asked "what's doing the work here?"
- Understands the meaning even if they can't explain the rule

**Assessment method:** Present the concept in a narrative or immersion encounter. Ask comprehension questions. The student doesn't need to name the pattern — they need to understand what it means.

**Threshold:** The student correctly interprets the concept in 3+ different contexts across 2+ sessions.

### 2. Production

**Definition:** The student can use the concept correctly when prompted or when the conversational context calls for it.

**Evidence:**
- Constructs grammatically correct sentences using the pattern
- Chooses the right form when given a production challenge
- May need a moment to recall, but gets there

**Assessment method:** Conversation encounters where the scenario requires the concept. Production challenges with specific prompts. Watch for correct conjugation, particle choice, and word order.

**Threshold:** The student correctly produces the concept in 3+ different contexts. Minor errors in adjacent elements (e.g., correct grammar pattern but wrong particle) still count as production mastery for the target concept.

### 3. Contextual Use

**Definition:** The student uses the concept naturally and appropriately without being prompted. They choose it because the situation calls for it, not because they know it's the lesson topic.

**Evidence:**
- Uses the pattern in conversation without being steered toward it
- Selects the right form among alternatives (e.g., chooses ~tara~ over ~ba~ when the context favors it)
- Uses the concept in encounters that target a different concept entirely

**Assessment method:** Track usage in conversation and production encounters that target OTHER concepts. If the student naturally uses a previously learned pattern while working on something new, that's contextual use evidence.

**Threshold:** Unprompted correct use in 2+ encounters targeting different concepts.

### 4. Transfer

**Definition:** The student can apply the concept in novel contexts, extend it to new situations, or combine it with other patterns creatively.

**Evidence:**
- Uses the pattern in an unexpected but valid construction
- Combines it with other patterns to express complex ideas (e.g., conditional + causative-passive)
- Self-corrects mid-sentence, showing active monitoring
- Explains the concept to Sensei using their own mental model (not textbook language)

**Assessment method:** Production challenges with open-ended prompts. Conversation encounters with unexpected twists. Reflection encounters where the student articulates their understanding.

**Threshold:** Creative or novel correct use in 2+ different contexts, OR self-correction in real-time, OR accurate metacognitive explanation.

## Observable Signals

Sensei watches for these signals during every interaction:

### Positive Signals (Mastery Advancing)

- **Self-correction:** The student starts to say one thing, stops, and corrects themselves. This means the correct form is competing with the incorrect one — and winning. Highly positive signal.
- **Hesitation before correct choice:** A brief pause before selecting the right particle or conjugation. The student is running a comparison. Less advanced than automatic production, but shows the distinction is active.
- **Creative extension:** The student uses a pattern in a way Sensei hasn't demonstrated. They've abstracted the rule from the examples. This is the strongest signal of acquisition.
- **Speed increase:** The student parses or produces the pattern faster than in previous encounters. Automaticity is developing.
- **Spontaneous use:** The student uses a pattern that isn't the current lesson topic. They're integrating it into their active repertoire.
- **Metalinguistic awareness:** The student asks WHY a pattern works the way it does, or notices a similarity to another pattern. They're building a mental model, not just memorizing.

### Concerning Signals (Mastery Stalling or Regressing)

- **Consistent hesitation without resolution:** The student always pauses at the same point and still gets it wrong. The distinction isn't forming.
- **Avoidance:** The student constructs sentences that work around the target concept. They're solving the communication problem without the target tool. Effective communication, but not acquisition.
- **Overgeneralization:** The student applies the pattern where it doesn't belong (e.g., using ~te shimau~ for every completed action). They've acquired the form but not the semantic boundaries.
- **L1 transfer errors:** The student produces Japanese that follows English structure. "I to my friend spoke" instead of natural Japanese word order. Persistent L1 transfer suggests the underlying concept hasn't been internalized.
- **Rote repetition:** The student can produce the pattern only in the exact context where they learned it. No transfer, no variation. They've memorized, not acquired.

### Neutral Signals (Expected at Stage)

- **Slow but correct production:** Normal for concepts recently reaching the production dimension. Speed comes with practice.
- **Correct in simple contexts, fails in complex ones:** Normal progression. The concept is acquired for simple cases; complex cases need more encounters.
- **Knows the rule but produces incorrectly:** The learning-acquisition gap. Knowledge is there but automaticity isn't. More input needed, not more explanation.

## Assessment Through Conversation

### The Invisible Test Principle

The student should never feel assessed. Every conversational turn is data, but the student experiences it as a conversation, not an exam.

**Techniques:**
- **Embedded questions:** Instead of "what particle goes here?" — present a scene where the student naturally needs to choose. Their choice IS the assessment.
- **Recast and observe:** If the student makes an error, Sensei recasts the sentence correctly in their response. If the student self-corrects in their next turn, that's assessment data (they noticed the recast).
- **Follow-up complications:** After the student succeeds with a simple version, add a complication. "Good — now what if it happened yesterday?" tests whether they can combine the target concept with past tense.
- **Open production prompts:** "What would you say to the guild master?" — the student's response reveals which patterns are in their active repertoire and which they avoid.

### Recording Mastery Events

After each encounter, Sensei records mastery observations as structured events:

```
Concept: te-form + shimau (completion/regret)
Dimension: Production
Signal: Student correctly used "tabete shimatta" in guild scene without prompting
Context: Conversation encounter targeting N4 conditionals (not shimau)
Assessment: Contextual use — unprompted correct use while targeting different concept
```

These events feed the mastery profile and inform the knowledge graph's "mastery level" for each concept-learner pair.

## Mastery Regression

Mastery can regress. This is normal and expected, especially for concepts that:
- Were recently acquired (not yet consolidated)
- Haven't been used in several sessions (decay)
- Conflict with a newly introduced concept (interference)

### Detection

- A concept previously at "contextual use" level is suddenly produced incorrectly
- The student hesitates on a concept they previously handled automatically
- Avoidance of a previously used pattern

### Response

Sensei does not alarm the student. Regression is normal. The response is:

1. Note the regression in the mastery profile
2. Design a brief encounter that revisits the concept in a new context
3. If regression persists across 2+ sessions, check for interference from a newly learned concept and address the conflict explicitly
4. If regression is consistent across multiple concepts, consider whether the student is overwhelmed and adjust pacing

### Confirmation of Regression vs. Normal Variation

A single failed use is not regression. Require:
- 2+ failures on a concept previously at production or contextual use level
- Failures across different encounter types (not just one bad day)
- Pattern of avoidance or substitution

Only after confirming regression does Sensei lower the mastery dimension in the profile.

## Reporting

Sensei maintains a running mastery profile for each student. The profile tracks:

- **Per concept:** Current mastery dimension (recognition / production / contextual use / transfer)
- **Per concept:** Number of successful and unsuccessful observations at each dimension
- **Per concept:** Last encounter date and type
- **Overall:** Distribution of concepts across dimensions (how many at recognition vs. production vs. contextual use vs. transfer)
- **Overall:** Active concept count (concepts currently being worked on)
- **Overall:** Acquisition rate (concepts reaching contextual use per week)
- **Overall:** Regression rate (concepts dropping a dimension per week)

The profile is stored as a Kotowari entity (LearnerMastery) and updated after each session. It is the primary input for concept sequencing and encounter design.
